# Do Now 8.D: Ask, Then Verify (Optional) 🎯

**Time:** 5 minutes
**Format:** No sandbox or tooling required, this activity is done mentally and captured in Zoom chat (or on the Mural board if the instructor directs).
**Mural:** https://app.mural.co/t/day10626/m/day10626/1790240815059/7cd9ba5bad037a5d50aa2ebcc1e724bb48b31d87

## Goal
By the end of this five-minute warm-up you will have taken one factual claim from an AI assistant, the worked question is "What is the current public comment period for the proposed rule?", and verified it against the authoritative source, the agency rulemaking page or its Federal Register entry. The artifact you produce is one structured verdict posted in chat: `TRUE/FALSE + date checked + URL + one sentence of evidence`, where the date matters because words like "current" and "latest" decay every single day. The instinct being built is the trust-but-verify reflex: an AI answer with a confident tone and no source is the most dangerous artifact in a government workflow, and no claim should feed a decision until a human has checked it against the page of record.

## Why it matters
AI assistants state outdated comment periods, superseded directives, and plausible-sounding deadlines with exactly the same confidence as true ones, and in government work, acting on a stale date has real consequences. Verifying one answer before trusting it is a core AI-at-work habit; doing it in chat makes the reasoning visible and easy to correct.

**Assets:** An approved AI assistant (or a sample AI answer provided by the instructor) plus one authoritative source (see `board-items.tsv`).

**🌐 With Copilot in the browser:** Ask Copilot the question, then verify on the official agency or Federal Register page, Copilot is the fast first pass; the authoritative site is the verdict.

## Run steps 🪜
1. Take the worked question from the board, "What is the current public comment period for the proposed rule?", or substitute one factual claim from your own program area.
2. Read the AI assistant's answer (use the sample answer the instructor shares if you do not have the approved assistant open, no tools are needed for this exercise).
3. Identify what would count as the source of truth for this claim: the agency rulemaking page, the Federal Register entry, or the regulations.gov docket, not another AI answer and not a search snippet.
4. Compare the claim against that source line by line: dates, docket numbers, and status, decide TRUE or FALSE, and note the date you checked, because "current" decays.
5. Post your verdict in Zoom chat using the template: `TRUE/FALSE + date checked + URL + one sentence of evidence`.
6. Read two other verdicts in chat; if someone's verdict contradicts yours against the same source, reply and reconcile the discrepancy before the debrief.

## Key takeaway 💡
Trust is a process, not a vibe, every AI answer that feeds a decision gets a verdict, a check date, a URL, and one sentence of evidence.

## Study further 📚
- [Federal Register](https://www.federalregister.gov/), the official daily journal of the U.S. government, where proposed rules and comment periods are published.
- [Regulations.gov](https://www.regulations.gov/), the official portal for rulemaking dockets and public comment submissions.
- [What are AI hallucinations?, IBM Think](https://www.ibm.com/think/topics/ai-hallucinations), clear explainer of why confident AI answers still need source verification.

## Common mistake to name ⚠️
Posting TRUE/FALSE with no URL or check date; a verdict without a source and a date is just one more unverified claim.

## If finished early ⏩
Verify a second claim from the same AI answer, did the assistant stay accurate across both, and what does that tell you about spot-checking versus full verification?
