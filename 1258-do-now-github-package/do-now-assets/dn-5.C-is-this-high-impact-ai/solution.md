# Sample / solution

## Why this approach works
Screening by who is affected when the model is wrong works because "high-impact" under OMB M-25-21 describes consequences for people, not the sophistication of the technology, and the label is the gate that triggers impact assessment, real-world testing, and meaningful human oversight. The screening question, does the output touch rights, benefits, or safety, is deliberately crude, because a gate that requires a risk workshop to operate will never get applied to the small use cases that quietly need it. The weaker instinct is ranking by how fancy the AI sounds; the failure mode is the H6 autocomplete getting review attention because it feels technical while the H1 eligibility recommender ships unscreened because it is "just a recommendation," even though a wrong H1 output can cost a family housing. The Maybe column is a feature, not a failure: naming the fact that would flip your vote is what turns a gut call into a defensible determination.

## A complete solution
Full screen: YES gets H1 (housing-benefits eligibility recommendation: touches rights and benefits) and H3 (child-welfare hotline triage: safety and rights impact, a human must decide). NO gets H2 (internal meeting-notes summarizer: low impact if no decisions are automated from it) and H6 (code-comment autocomplete: low rights impact, though you still protect the code). MAYBE gets H4 (public FAQ drafter: content risk, not high-impact by itself) and H5 (fire-safety inspection flagging: resource allocation with a safety effect). Chat post: `YES: H1, H3 | NO: H2, H6 | MAYBE: H4, H5`, with the trigger sentence `H1: Yes, affects rights/benefits; needs strong review`.

H5 is the sticky worth arguing about. The wrong instinct is to call it No because it is "internal operations," just a priority list for inspectors, and no resident ever sees it. The correct instinct is to follow the consequence: if the model ranks a dangerous building low, the inspection happens late, and the safety effect lands on occupants who never consented to the screening. That makes H5 a Maybe leaning Yes, and the fact that flips it fully is whether the flags determine the inspection order or merely inform an inspector who can override. H4 earns its Maybe for the opposite reason: drafting FAQs from approved content is a content risk, wrong tone, stale policy, and those are handled by editorial review, but it flips to Yes the day the FAQ answers questions about benefit eligibility, because then a wrong answer touches benefits. H2 stays No only while the summaries inform meetings; my edge-case comment on the board: "H2 flips to high-impact if summaries feed a personnel decision." The board is done when every sticky has a column and at least one Yes carries its trigger, because that sentence is the one you will repeat to oversight.
