# Sample / solution

## Why this approach works
The pair works because accuracy is audience-relative: a sentence is only correct if it is correct for its reader. The CIO version earns its place by leading with what an executive is accountable for, efficiency, risk controls, and measurable service outcomes, in one sentence with no hype words like "leverage" or "transform." The citizen version earns its place with plain words and the honest caveat that people still check the output; dropping that caveat is not simplification, it is misrepresentation. The common failure, writing one sentence and calling it both, fails each audience in a different direction: jargon to the citizen, and a missing human-check caveat to the CIO. Writing the two side by side is the fastest way to feel that difference, and it is the same switching you will do briefing upward, testifying outward, and writing the memo in between.

## A complete solution
For generative AI, a complete chat post looks like this:

`CIO: Generative AI drafts and summarizes routine text so staff time shifts from writing to reviewing, but it only pays off if we wrap it in risk controls and measure the service outcomes, not the novelty.`

`CITIZEN: It is a tool that writes a first draft or a short summary for staff, and a person still checks it before anything is sent to you or decided about you.`

Both hit their cues: the CIO sentence names efficiency, risk controls, and measurable service outcomes; the citizen sentence says what the tool does in plain words and keeps the honest caveat about human checking. If the instructor names classic ML instead, the matched pair is:

`CIO: Classic ML predicts scores and classifications from our historical data with a known error rate, which makes it cheap to run and auditable, but only as good as the data behind it and the error we are willing to accept.`

`CITIZEN: It is a pattern-spotter that gives a case a score or a category based on what happened in the past, and a person still makes the final call.`

The classic ML cues check out the same way: prediction from historical data with defined error on the CIO side, a pattern-spotter that scores or classifies cases on the citizen side. Each version is one sentence, which is the discipline the exercise is really practicing.
