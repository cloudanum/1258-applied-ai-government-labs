# Do Now 1.B: Explain It to a CIO, Then to a Citizen 🎯

**Time:** 5 minutes
**Format:** No sandbox or tooling required — this activity is done mentally and captured in Zoom chat (or on the Mural board if you are in the room).
**Mural:** https://app.mural.co/t/day10626/m/day10626/1790240784145/1d6c3055140c3f3d85f9ef06e435ebefc23eba24

## Goal
By the end of this five-minute warm-up you will have taken one course concept — generative AI or classic ML, as named by the instructor — and explained it twice: one sentence for a CIO and one sentence for a citizen, both posted in the capture channel. The artifact is a matched pair of sentences about the same idea that sound nothing alike yet are both accurate. The skill underneath is audience switching, the most-used communication skill in government AI work: executives need efficiency, risk controls, and measurable service outcomes, while the public needs plain words, no jargon, and an honest statement of what the tool does and who still checks it. If you can do this on demand, you can brief upward, testify outward, and write the memo in between.

## Why it matters
Government AI work lives or dies on translation: the same system must be defensible in a budget meeting and understandable on a public web page. Doing it in chat makes the reasoning visible and easy to correct — you will see twenty other pairs and immediately spot which ones drift into jargon or condescension.

**Assets:** One course concept chosen by the instructor (see `board-items.tsv` for the CIO/citizen cues).

**🌐 With Copilot in the browser:** Draft both versions in Copilot, then edit them yourself — Copilot is good at audience switching, but the skill you are practicing is judging whether each version is right for its reader.

## Run steps 🪜
1. Note the concept the instructor names — generative AI or classic ML.
2. Draft your CIO sentence: lead with what it means for the agency — efficiency, risk controls, measurable service outcomes — and keep it to one sentence.
3. Draft your citizen sentence: plain words, no acronyms — for classic ML, "a pattern-spotter that scores or classifies cases" rather than "a supervised model".
4. Check the pair against the cues in `board-items.tsv`: does the citizen version keep the honest caveat that people still check the output?
5. Post both sentences together in Zoom chat, labeled `CIO:` and `CITIZEN:`, so the room can compare pairs at a glance — or add both as one sticky on the Mural board if you are in the room.
6. Read three other pairs in chat and reply to one where the citizen sentence still hides jargon — name the word you would replace.
7. Be ready to read your pair aloud; the instructor will pick one or two contrasting pairs to discuss.

## Key takeaway 💡
The same concept must survive two audiences: a CIO version that speaks in outcomes, cost, and risk, and a citizen version in plain words with an honest caveat — if either one is wrong, the explanation is wrong.

## Common mistake to name ⚠️
Writing one sentence and calling it both — a CIO sentence full of "leverage" and "transform" is jargon to a citizen, and a citizen sentence with no caveat about human checking is misleading to a CIO.
