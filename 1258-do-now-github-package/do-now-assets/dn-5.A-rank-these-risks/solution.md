# Sample / solution

## Why this approach works
Ranking by likelihood and impact in your own agency's context works because no agency can mitigate all eight risks, so the two you fund first quietly define your real security posture, and oversight will ask why you chose them. Headline anxiety ranks by fear: data poisoning and model theft sound cinematic, so they win the fear contest while prompt injection, which arrives free with every submitted document, goes unfunded. The grid forces the honest question for each sticky: is it likely here, and how bad is it here, and the facilitator questions supply the context that decides ("who controls training data and labels?" moves R1's likelihood more than any headline). The weaker instinct is ranking by what made the news; the failure mode is a mitigation budget spent on low-likelihood attacks while an authorized user exports data off the books and nobody logged it.

## A complete solution
One defensible board for a document-handling agency: R1 data poisoning at low-to-medium likelihood, high impact (likelihood hinges on who controls training data and labels); R2 evasion at medium likelihood, medium impact; R3 prompt injection at high likelihood, high impact; R4 model extraction at low likelihood, medium impact for an internal, rate-limited service; R5 membership inference at low-to-medium likelihood, high impact if sensitive records were in training data; R6 denial of service at medium likelihood, medium impact with quota and fallback in place; R7 insider misuse at high likelihood, high impact; R8 bias and fairness failure at medium-to-high likelihood, high impact.

Chat post: `FUND FIRST: R3, R7, because both pair high likelihood with high impact in our context: every submitted document reaches the prompt, so R3's likelihood is high and hijacked instructions make its impact high, and R7 needs no attacker at all, just one authorized user exporting data or changing parameters where changes are not logged and approved.`

The tricky item is R1, and it deserves a full narration. The wrong instinct is to fund it first because poisoning a fraud model so it waves bad transactions through is the scariest story on the board. The correct call is to ask the facilitator question before ranking. If your labels come from audited case outcomes that a vendor cannot touch, likelihood drops and R1 stays in the high-impact column without making FUND FIRST; if a vendor labels data you never review, the honest ranking changes and R1 jumps. That is the lesson: the ranking is not right or wrong in the abstract, it is defensible when it names the mechanism, the context, and the deciding factor. My disagreement comment on a colleague who funded R1 and R4: "R4 assumes an attacker with a large query budget against a public endpoint; are responses rate-limited and monitored? Ours are, so I put R4's likelihood below R7's, where the misuse needs nothing but an account we already issued."
