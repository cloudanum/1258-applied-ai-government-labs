# Sample / solution

## Why this approach works
Every dashboard is an argument, and checking the axis, the denominator, and the definition before believing the trend is what makes the critique defensible. The denominator critique deserves the vote: 98 resolved in June is success if 100 requests arrived and a crisis if 300 did, and no axis fix can rescue a number whose meaning depends on data that is not shown.

## A complete solution
Three red stickies and one green. Red, misleading: the y-axis starts at 80, so the climb from 82 in January to 98 in June fills the chart like a rocket; on a zero-based axis the same bars show a modest rise that actually dipped from 85 in February to 84 in March, and the "Soars!" headline would have to earn its word. Red, missing: the denominator. Resolved out of how many received? With no requests-received count, no backlog, no channel or geography breakdown, and no uncertainty interval, the chart cannot answer the only question a service dashboard exists for: are we keeping up? Red, unclear: "resolved" is undefined; closed ticket, satisfied citizen, and SLA met are three different numbers wearing one label, and until the definition is pinned the trend is uninterpretable. A side note worth a comment: the category pie adds little, every slice sits between 13% and 15%, so it shows no trend and no priority.

Green sticky: the month-over-month resolution trend across six months is real signal worth keeping; once the axis starts at 0 and a denominator appears, this panel becomes the useful core of the dashboard.

The vote lands on the missing denominator, matching the sample's own caption hint. The chat fallback post reads: "worst issue: no denominator, we cannot tell whether 98 resolved is keeping up with demand | one fix: plot requests received next to resolved each month, or show resolved as a percent of received, on a zero-based axis." If a colleague votes for the truncated axis instead, the defense is priority: the axis exaggerates a trend that exists, while the missing denominator conceals whether the trend is good news at all, and a hidden crisis outranks an exaggerated success.
